Salmonella enterica uses type III secretion systems (T3SSs) to inject effector proteins into host cells during infection. Specifically, the T3SSs are molecular syringe-like machines that deliver virulence factors into host cells, allowing Salmonella to subvert host cell processes and maintain an appropriate intracellular niche (Hotinger et al., 2021; Lam et al., 2021). The two type III secretion systems encoded on Salmonella pathogenicity island 1 (SPI-1) and SPI-2 are essential for delivering over 40 effectors to subvert host cell functions during infection (Schilder et al., 2016). These effector proteins, such as SptP, play a crucial role in maintaining the survival of Salmonella within phagocytic cells (Johnson et al., 2017).

The T3SSs are composed of two main components: the needle complex and the effector translocation apparatus. The needle complex is responsible for injecting the effector proteins into host cells, while the effector translocation apparatus controls the translocon-to-effector switch, allowing the effector proteins to be released into the host cell cytoplasm (Yu et al., 2018). In addition, the T3SSs also interact with other host cell components, such as glucosylceramidase A, which affects the lipidome and transcriptome of mammalian host cells (Aguilera-Herce et al., 2023).

Overall, the type III secretion system is a critical virulence factor for Salmonella enterica, allowing it to manipulate host cell processes and maintain an intracellular niche that favors its survival and replication.
