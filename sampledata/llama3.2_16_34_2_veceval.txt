Salmonella enterica uses a type III secretion system (T3SS) to inject effectors into the host cell cytosol, which plays a crucial role in its pathogenicity. This T3SS is composed of specialized nanomachines that facilitate the injection of effector proteins into the host cells, allowing Salmonella to manipulate the host's cellular environment and evade the immune system (Hotinger et al., 2021). The type III secretion system is essential for Salmonella's ability to invade host cells and establish a successful infection. By injecting effector proteins into host cells, Salmonella can modulate various cellular processes, including cell signaling pathways and immune responses.

According to Johnson et al. (2017), the T3SS effector SptP of Salmonella enterica Serovar Typhi is an example of how this system functions in the pathogen's ability to manipulate host cells. The study highlights the importance of understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying the type III secretion system, which can inform the development of novel therapeutic strategies against Salmonella infections.

In addition, research has shown that the T3SS plays a critical role in regulating the translocon-to-effector switch, allowing for the precise control of effector protein delivery into host cells (Yu et al., 2018). This complex system is essential for Salmonella's virulence and highlights the importance of understanding its molecular mechanisms.

Overall, the type III secretion system is a critical component of Salmonella enterica's pathogenicity, enabling the bacterium to inject effector proteins into host cells during infection.
